## Quick-Reference Formula & Feature Cheat-Sheet

Excel statistical functions: AVERAGE(number1,[number2],...) = mean; MEDIAN(number1,[number2],...) = middle value; MODE(number1,[number2],...) = most frequent value; MAX(...)-MIN(...) = range; VAR.P(number1,[number2],...) = variance of a full population; QUARTILE(array,quart) = quartile value, where quart 0=minimum, 1=25th percentile (Q1), 2=median/50th percentile (Q2), 3=75th percentile (Q3), 4=maximum.

Excel lookup/validation functions: VLOOKUP(lookup_value, table_array, col_index_num, [range_lookup]) looks up a value in the first column of a range and returns a value from a specified column in the same row; range_lookup FALSE = exact match, TRUE/omitted = approximate match. IF(logical_test, [value_if_true], [value_if_false]) branches on a condition; IF statements can be nested for multi-tier logic, and combined with AND/OR for compound conditions.

Excel text-cleaning functions: LEFT(text,[num_chars]) takes characters from the start of a string; RIGHT(text,[num_chars]) takes characters from the end; MID(text,start_num,num_chars) takes characters from a specified starting position; LEN(text) counts the number of characters; FIND(find_text, within_text,[start_num]) returns the numeric position of one text string inside another (case-sensitive, returns #VALUE! if not found). LEFT and LEN, or LEFT and FIND, are commonly nested together to strip a variable number of trailing characters from text.

Pivot table "Show Values As" custom calculations: % of Grand Total, % of Column Total, % of Row Total, Difference From, % Difference From, Running Total In, Rank Smallest to Largest, Rank Largest to Smallest.

Power BI: two main visualization types for progress-tracking are the KPI visual (base measure vs goal, shown as a tick/green if the goal is met, an exclamation mark/red if it is not, and yellow if it exactly matches) and the Gauge visual (a circular arc from minimum to maximum showing current progress toward the target as a shaded arc with a target marker line).

## LESSON 1 – KEY CONCEPTS (book pages 2–15)

### 1.1 Types of Data Analytics (pages 2–7)
Organisations collect and process vast amounts of data from an ever-growing range of sources, including mobile devices, online platforms, payment systems, cameras, GPS systems, wireless sensors, and legacy systems. This data may arrive in many different formats, and organisations increasingly rely on data analytics to translate these large, varied data streams into meaningful and useful insights that support their organisational goals.

Data analytics itself refers to the techniques and processes used to collect, organise, and examine data sets so that meaningful and useful information can be produced from them. The use of data analytics has grown rapidly in recent years thanks to technological advances, increased computing power, and better data storage capabilities — and organisations that adopt it well can gain a real competitive advantage, improve performance, and increase profits.

There are two broad categories of data analytics based on the nature of the data being examined. Quantitative analytics is used to analyse information that can be measured and expressed numerically — for example profits, costs, sales figures, the number of repeat purchases, or the number of complaints. It typically relies on structured techniques to gather data (such as surveys, business reports, and polls) and applies mathematical and statistical methods to interpret the numbers gathered, giving broad, generalised, and objectively-assessed insights. Qualitative analytics, by contrast, is used to analyse information about attitudes, opinions, and behaviours — things that cannot simply be measured as numbers, such as a customer's opinion of a product or their feelings about a company's reputation. It uses unstructured or semi-structured techniques such as focus groups, discussions, interviews, and open-text survey responses, and the results are more narrow and specific but also more subjective, since they depend on interpretation of language and are harder to generalise. A useful way to think about it: qualitative analytics can often provide the "why" behind patterns that quantitative analytics reveals only as numbers.

Beyond that split, data analytics activities are usually classified into four progressively more advanced types, based on the purpose they serve and the sophistication of the analysis:

Descriptive analytics is the most basic type and is used to find out what happened in the past. It summarises raw or historical data from multiple sources using descriptive statistics such as arithmetic mean, median, and percentage. Descriptive analytics can be used to create management reports that provide insight into past performance — for example to compare actual sales figures against a sales target — but on its own it doesn't explain why the results occurred.

Diagnostic analytics goes a step further, looking to find out why something happened in the past. It involves a deeper look at the data to understand root causes and the factors that contributed to a particular outcome, using techniques such as drill-down, data discovery, and probability/likelihood analysis of outcomes. A practical example given in the book is social media marketing: diagnostic analytics could be used to assess engagement levels through numbers of posts, mentions, followers, fans, page views, reviews, and pins, to understand what worked and what didn't in a past campaign.

Predictive analytics is used to identify what is likely to happen in the future. It uses the findings of descriptive and diagnostic analytics to forecast the probability of a future outcome, essentially building statistical or mathematical models of current and historical data. Its accuracy always depends on the quality and completeness of the underlying data. A business example given is a bank using predictive analytics to estimate the likelihood that a customer will repay a loan on time, based on patterns in that customer's history.

Prescriptive analytics is the most advanced and complex type, used to identify the best action to take now to influence a future outcome — it's about avoiding problems that may arise in the future, or making the best use of current trends. Prescriptive analytics combines findings from predictive analytics with historical and transactional data, real-time data feeds, mathematical models, and business rules, often using techniques such as optimisation, simulation, and decision-analysis modelling. Because it is more complex and resource-intensive, prescriptive analytics tends to be used mainly by larger organisations, for example to optimise production scheduling and inventory so that the right products are delivered to the right places at the right time, maximising the customer experience.

The book includes a worked case study article about a well-known fast-fashion retailer (referred to in the book via a business case example) that captures data from point-of-sale terminals, e-commerce transactions, customer surveys, RFID tags in clothing, and in-store cameras, and combines it with data gathered from Instagram, other social media, and customer surveys. This combined data feeds a 24-hour analysis cycle in which the retailer processes new release data twice a week to decide what to ship to stores, generating weekly predictions for every SKU (stock-keeping unit) so it can adapt quickly if a design isn't selling — illustrating descriptive analytics (what sold), diagnostic analytics (why it sold or didn't), predictive analytics (what will sell), and prescriptive analytics (what to ship next) working together in a single real business process.

### 1.2 Business Benefits (pages 8–9)
Knowing how to use and interpret data helps businesses reach their goals and stay ahead of the competition. The book groups the main benefits of data analytics into four themes.

Identifying patterns and trends: analysing large sets of data containing varying data types allows businesses to uncover hidden patterns in the data, such as customer preferences and behaviour (for example from loyalty-card and online purchasing data, which can support personalised recommendations and targeted advertising), popular products and market trends in retail and supply-chain management, and even to help organisations that give away services for free (in exchange for personal data) generate revenue from targeted marketing using that same data.

Improving efficiency: the insight gained from data analytics allows businesses to automate and optimise their processes. The book's example is a chain of hotels analysing historic data to predict when their hotels will be busiest, so they can allocate staff and resources more efficiently and reduce costs.

Supporting decision-making: access to large volumes of analysed data helps managers and decision-makers make quicker, more informed choices and set more effective business strategies, using regularly-updated dashboards and reports that display up-to-date and/or summarised performance metrics. Examples given include an HR service using data analytics to predict the impact of a proposed policy on employee performance and well-being before rolling it out, and a healthcare provider using data analytics to predict patients' medical needs to allocate resources more effectively.

Presenting information effectively: data presented in easy-to-understand formats such as tables, charts, maps, and dashboards helps businesses interpret and understand their data better, and using visual data in reports and dashboards makes organisations more agile, since it helps people find insights faster and make decisions more quickly.

### 1.3 The Data Analysis Process (pages 10–12)
Using data analytics to gain business intelligence can be challenging, and the best approach depends on the type of data analytics being used and how the business intends to implement it. It can include deciding the question you are trying to answer, deciding when you have the data ready, getting the data ready for analysis (cleaning and transforming it), analysing the data, and interpreting and using the results.

A widely-used framework for this, known as data mining, breaks the data analysis process into six phases, which typically follow a cyclical order: (1) Business understanding — understand the business goals and what information is needed to achieve them, for example setting a target to increase customer retention by 10% from last year; (2) Data understanding — determine the data requirements, review the data you have acquired to establish whether there are any data management or quality problems that need to be addressed, involving collecting, exploring and assessing the data; (3) Data preparation — prepare the data for the modelling phase by constructing the final data set to use in your analysis from the initial raw data, which involves selecting, cleaning, and transforming (constructing and integrating) the data; (4) Modelling — select and apply appropriate data modelling tools and techniques, including selecting modelling techniques, designing tests, building models, and assessing those models — this phase usually involves trial and error and often takes multiple iterations; (5) Evaluation — evaluate the results of the model against the business success criteria you set in phase 1; (6) Deployment — use the results to meet the original business goals, which includes planning the deployment of the model into the business, reporting the results, and monitoring and maintaining that deployment over time.

A note in the book clarifies the scope of this Foundation-level module: the focus in this course is specifically on the data understanding and data preparation phases, and on the associated tools in Microsoft Excel and Power BI (rather than the modelling/deployment phases, which belong to more advanced data science work).

### 1.4 Data Protection Considerations (page 13)
Data protection is an area of law designed to protect the privacy rights of individuals in relation to the processing of their personal data. Personal data is typically defined as data that contains identifiers that can be used to identify an individual, and data protection law governs how organisations must use personal data and stay compliant when analysing data that contains it.

Data protection regulations vary across the world, so when analysing data that contains identifiers, organisations need to ensure compliance with the relevant data protection law in their jurisdiction. The book uses the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) as its example of a data protection regulation: GDPR came into effect on 25th May 2018 in the European Economic Area (EEA), applies to organisations established within any EU/EEA member state, and also applies outside that area to any organisation that offers goods or services to, or monitors the behaviour of, individuals within the EU/EEA. Among GDPR's core requirements, personal data that an organisation processes must be: processed lawfully, fairly, and in a transparent manner; only collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes; adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary for the processing purpose (data minimisation); accurate and kept up to date; kept for no longer than is necessary; and stored securely.

Because complying with data protection laws can require significant effort, an organisation analysing personal data may decide it is not actually necessary to achieve its analysis objectives to use directly identifiable personal data at all — in which case it makes sense to anonymise the data to avoid unnecessary compliance risk and reduce the chance of a data breach exposing identifiable individuals. Anonymising personal data means removing direct identifiers such as names and addresses, or reducing detail such as replacing an exact date of birth with just the birth year, so that an individual can no longer be identified from the remaining data while it can still be analysed in aggregate.

## LESSON 2 – STATISTICAL ANALYSIS (book pages 16–31)

In this lesson you will learn how to: describe measures of central tendency of a data set; calculate central tendency values of a data set; describe some measures of variation of a data set; calculate the variation of a data set.

### 2.1 Summary Statistics Introduction (pages 16–17)
To make sense of, and interpret, large data sets of quantitative data, the data needs to be summarised or described in some way — statistical analysis is a way of summarising or describing data to highlight typical values in the data, as well as how these values vary.

Statistical analysis techniques for summarising or describing data are known as summary or descriptive statistics, and there are two main technique families: measures of central tendency (averages), and measures of variation.

Measures of central tendency are summary values that describe a data set by identifying the middle or central position within the data. Three common measures of central tendency are mean, median, and mode. Each calculates the location of the central point differently, so which one is most appropriate depends on the data set and what you want to find out.

Measures of variation are statistical techniques for interpreting and summarising large sets of quantitative data by examining the distribution of the data — rather than showing how similar the data are (as central tendency does), measures of variation describe how data varies or differs, and are also referred to as measures of dispersion, distribution, or spread. Three common measures of variation are range, variance, and quartiles.

Using a measure of central tendency and the relevant measure of variation together for a data set provides a much better overview of the data than using either alone. In data analysis, this type of statistical analysis can be the first step in describing the data, before more complex analysis.

A worked case example poses the (deliberately playful) newspaper-style question "are our feet really getting bigger?" — a shoe retailer has reportedly seen an increase in sales of larger sizes. Before jumping to conclusions, the notes recommend checking what is meant by "average" foot size: is a single average value being reported, or a range of values? And how far apart are the different sizes being classed as "average"? This illustrates why you need to understand which measure of central tendency and which measure of variation is being used before you can properly interpret a reported average.

### 2.2 Measures of Central Tendency (pages 18–19)
The mean is the most popular and most commonly-referenced measure of central tendency — the everyday "average." It is calculated by summing every value in the data set and then dividing by the number of values in that data set. Because it incorporates every value, any change to any single value in the data set will affect the mean.

Worked example: gross profit figures for 10 fashion-retail stores are given as $150,000, $180,000, $160,000, $140,000, $150,000, $150,000, $120,000, $170,000, $190,000, and $130,000, totalling $1,540,000. Dividing this total by the 10 stores gives a mean gross profit of $154,000. This mean value can be visualised on a column chart with a horizontal reference line drawn across the bars at the $154,000 mark, making it easy to see at a glance which stores sit above or below the mean.

The book cautions that the mean may not always reflect the central position well, especially when the data set has outliers — values that are unusually large or small compared to the rest of the set — since the mean value will be skewed by these outliers. A second worked example uses service response times (in minutes) across 10 customers of a restaurant: 5, 8, 6, 4, 5, 5, 2, 7, 19, and 13 minutes, totalling 74 minutes across 10 customers, giving a mean of 7.4 minutes. However, inspecting the raw data shows most values actually cluster between 2 and 8 minutes, with 19 and 13 minutes being clear outliers that pull the mean upward — so quoting "7.4 minutes" alone somewhat overstates the typical wait a customer actually experiences.

The median is the middle value in an ordered (ranked) data set — it splits a data set exactly in half, so that 50% of the values lie above it and 50% lie below it. Because it depends only on position, not magnitude, the median is far less affected by outliers or skewed data than the mean. Note: to calculate the median manually, you must first arrange the data set in ascending order before locating the middle value. If there is an odd number of values, the median is simply the middle value; if there is an even number of values (as in the worked examples), the median is the average (mean) of the two middle values.

Worked example: a small business's sales over 10 days are 17,000; 18,000; 16,000; 14,000; 15,000; 14,000; 12,000; 17,000; 19,000; and 13,000. Sorted ascending, the 5th and 6th values are 15,000 and 16,000, so the median = (15,000 + 16,000) / 2 = 15,500. The book uses this to illustrate a common business scenario — a business with day-to-day sales fluctuations (such as a spike on a promotional "Cyber Monday"-type event) may prefer to report the median rather than the mean of a typical day's sales, since the median is less distorted by one or two unusually high or low days.

The mode is the value that occurs most frequently in the data set — unlike mean and median, the mode is not affected by extreme values, and it can be used for either numerical or qualitative (categorical) data. If no value repeats, the data set has no mode; some data sets can have more than one mode (bimodal, if two values are tied for most frequent, or multimodal, if three or more are tied). The book notes the mode is particularly useful in retail-type contexts — for example, a bookstore might use the mode to identify which specific book is most in demand.

Worked example: a customer-satisfaction survey collects ratings on a scale of 1 (lowest) to 5 (highest) from 10 restaurant customers: 5, 4, 5, 3, 5, 4, 5, 3, 5, 2. Tallying the frequency of each rating value shows that "5" was given by 6 of the 10 respondents — more than any other rating — so the mode of this data set is 5.

### 2.3 Calculating Central Tendency (pages 22–23)
Spreadsheet applications provide built-in functions for calculating each of the central tendency values: AVERAGE(number1,[number2],...) calculates the mean of a set of values; MEDIAN(number1,[number2],...) finds the median (middle number) of a set of values in a data range; MODE(number1,[number2],...) finds the mode (the most frequently occurring number) in a numeric data set. In each case the arguments can be a number or a cell reference that refers to numeric values.

Follow the steps on pages 22–24 (opening the Orders1.xlsx workbook and entering the three formulas, e.g. into cells G4, G6, and G8 referencing an amount column C2:C16) to see this applied to a small order-tracking data set of order amounts with statuses like Delivered, Cancelled, and In transit — this produced a worked mean of $137, median of $120, and mode of $130 for that particular data set. Note that, unlike the manual calculation process, Excel's MEDIAN function does not require you to sort the data range first — it does that internally.

### 2.4 Measures of Variation (pages 24–25)
Measures of variation can be used in conjunction with measures of central tendency to give a more complete picture of a data set. Each of these measures is used to describe the dispersion/scatter of values in a distribution.

Range is the most basic measure of variation — it is the difference between the maximum and minimum values in the data set (Range = Maximum − Minimum), so it describes how dispersed or spread out a data set is using just its two extreme values. Because it is based only on the two most extreme values, range is very sensitive to outliers. Worked example: for 10 retail stores' gross profit figures, the minimum is $120,000 and the maximum is $190,000, giving a range of $70,000.

The book presents a two-store comparison to build intuition about why range and other variation measures matter: Store A's five daily sales representative contract counts are 10, 8, 6, 4, and 2 (mean = 6); Store B's are 6, 6, 6, 6, and 6 (mean = 6, i.e. identical to Store A); Store C's are 7, 6, 6, 6, and 5 (mean = 6, again identical). All three data sets share exactly the same mean of 6, yet they are clearly very differently distributed — this is precisely the kind of situation where a measure of central tendency alone is misleading, and you need a measure of variation to distinguish them.

Variance is a more sophisticated measure that quantifies how far each value in a data set is, on average, from the mean — the closer each individual value is to the mean, the smaller (more representative/tighter) the variance; if every value equalled the mean exactly, variance would be zero; the further individual values sit from the mean, the greater (less representative/more spread out) the variance. Technically, variance is calculated as the average of the squared differences from the mean (squaring removes negative signs so that deviations above and below the mean don't cancel out). Applying this to the Sales Rep A/B/C example: Sales Rep A's variance calculates out to 8, Sales Rep B's to 13.2, and Sales Rep C's to 0.4 — even though all three had the same mean of 6 contracts, Sales Rep C's volume is clearly the most consistent/representative (lowest variance), Sales Rep A is more variable, and Sales Rep B is the most volatile of the three (highest variance).

Quartiles are values that divide an ordered data set into quarters, and are often used in sales and survey analysis to divide a population into groups. The three key quartiles are: Q1 (the first/lower quartile) = the median of the lower half of the data set, i.e. the value at the 25th percentile; Q2 (the second quartile, also known simply as the median) = the value at the 50th percentile; Q3 (the third/upper quartile) = the median of the upper half of the data set, i.e. the value at the 75th percentile. There is a formula for finding the rank position of each quartile within an ordered data set of n values: Q1 position = (n+1)/4; Q2 (median) position = (n+1)/2; Q3 position = 3(n+1)/4.

Worked example: 12 daily online order quantities in the first two weeks after a product launch are 16, 14, 18, 4, 17, 10, 11, 12, 4, 3, 4, and 18. With n=12, Q2 (the median) works out to 10.5, taking the average of the 6th and 7th ranked values. Q1 works out to 4 (the average of the 3rd and 4th ranked values), and Q3 works out to 15 (the average of the 9th and 10th ranked values) in the example calculation shown.

### 2.5 Calculating Variation (pages 30–31)
Spreadsheet applications also provide built-in functions for the measures of variation: the MAX and MIN functions are used together to calculate the range, with the formula pattern MAX(number1,[number2],...) − MIN(number1,[number2],...); the VAR.P(number1,[number2],...) function finds the variance in an entire population (arguments can be numbers, or arrays/references containing numbers); and the QUARTILE(array,quart) function finds the quartile for a particular data set, where the array argument refers to the range of cells containing the numeric data you want to analyse, and the quart argument is a number from 0 to 4 specifying which return value you want (0 = minimum value, 1 = first quartile/25th percentile, 2 = median/50th percentile, 3 = third quartile/75th percentile, 4 = maximum value).

Follow the steps on pages 30–31 (opening the Orders2.xlsx workbook and entering formulas such as =MAX(C2:C16)-MIN(C2:C16) for range, =VAR.P(C2:C16) for variance, and =QUARTILE(C2:C16,1), =QUARTILE(C2:C16,2), =QUARTILE(C2:C16,3) for the three quartiles) to reproduce the worked variation-measures summary shown in the book, which for that order-amount data set produced a range of $210, a variance of 3586, and quartiles Q1=95, Q2 (median)=120, and Q3=137.5.

## LESSON 3 – IMPORTING DATA SETS (book pages 33–54)

In this section you will learn how to: import data into a spreadsheet application from a text file; import data into a spreadsheet application from a spreadsheet; import data into a spreadsheet application from a website table; import data into a spreadsheet application from a database table.

### 3.1 Importing Data Sets Introduction (page 34)
The data sets you want to analyse may be stored in various formats and programs and across systems, and may contain a few rows of data or thousands. Common sources include text files, spreadsheets, the web, and databases. You can bring data stored in these different formats into a spreadsheet application to perform further analysis, generate reports, and create dashboards. In some cases you will need to export data from the source program or system before importing it into Excel; in other cases you can import data from external sources directly from within Excel itself.

An important distinction the book draws is between connected and static imports: when you import data into Excel and make a permanent connection to the external source, the imported data can be updated (refreshed) if the source data changes — this is called a connected import. Alternatively, you can import data without making a permanent connection, in which case the imported data remains static regardless of any subsequent changes made to the external data source.

### 3.2 Importing Data from Text Files (pages 35–39)
In many cases when data is exported from other programs or systems, it is saved in a text file format. Two commonly-used text-file formats are the comma-separated values (CSV) text file, in which each column of text within a row has little or no formatting and each column is separated by delimiters (which can be another character such as a semi-colon or a tab space) with the extension .csv; and the delimited text file, which is similar to a CSV file except that columns are typically separated by the TAB character rather than another delimiter, and columns are usually all the same width so you may need to manually insert columns within the file — this file type usually has the extension .txt.

You can import data from a text file into Excel by importing the data as an external data range, which makes a permanent connection to the source file so the data can be refreshed; or you can import data from a text file by opening the file directly within Excel, which does not make a permanent connection, meaning imported data remains static regardless of changes to the external source.

Follow the steps on pages 35–39 for the full click-by-click walkthrough (this covers both the older Text Import Wizard workflow available in Excel 2016 as a standalone product — Data tab > Get External Data > From Text, selecting Delimited/Comma-separated and setting each column's data format and date format across the three-step wizard — and the newer Get & Transform / Power Query workflow available in Excel as part of Office 365 — Data tab > Get & Transform Data > From Text/CSV, using the Get Data preview window and Load To options). Both approaches ultimately import the customer contact/credit-limit data set from a Customer Data.csv file into the worksheet in table format, and either approach lets you refresh the imported data later by right-clicking the data set and choosing Refresh.

### 3.3 Importing Data from Spreadsheets (pages 40–43)
Sometimes you want to import data from one workbook into a different workbook — for example bringing existing sales or finance data from a separate workbook into your analysis workbook. Doing this via an external-data connection also lets you create a dynamic, refreshable connection between the two workbooks so imported figures stay up to date.

Follow the steps on pages 40–43 for the full click-by-click walkthrough (again shown for both older Excel 2016 stand-alone — Data tab > Get External Data > Existing Connections > Browse for More, then Select Table and Import Data dialog boxes — and newer Office 365 — Data tab > Get & Transform Data > Get Data > From File > From Workbook, using the Navigator dialog to pick which worksheet(s) to import and whether to load a Table, PivotTable Report, PivotChart, or Only Create Connection). ### 3.4 Importing Data from Website Tables (pages 44–48)
You may want to import information from web pages into Excel for analysis — for example importing stock information directly from a web page, or company sales figures from an internal company web page. Depending on your needs, you can import data that can refresh and update dynamically, or you can import and make the data static.

To import a web table into Excel you use the Web Query feature (in older Excel — Data tab > Get External Data > From Web, entering a web address such as the book's example URL http://www.icdl.org/exchangerates into the New Web Query dialog, clicking the arrow icon next to the desired table to select it, then Import) or the newer From Web feature under Get & Transform Data in Office 365, which uses a Navigator window to preview and select from the tables detected on the page before loading. Follow the steps on pages 45–49 for the full click-by-click walkthrough of both versions. Because web pages vary enormously in their formatting, the book notes that Excel will attempt to import the web data as it appears in the source table, but the less structured the web data is, the more manual formatting/clean-up will be required in Excel afterwards to make it ready for analysis. As with the other import types, you can refresh a connected web table later via right-click > Refresh.

### 3.5 Importing Data from Database Tables (pages 50–53)
A database management system (DBMS) provides a structured method of storing data by preventing redundancy and maintaining integrity. DBMSs are generally used to store large volumes of data in tables and have extensive querying abilities that break down into manageable chunks — however, the interface of a DBMS is limited, which means it can be time-consuming to manipulate data into meaningful reports directly within it. Importing database tables from a DBMS into Excel lets you more easily filter, chart, and analyse a data set, and produce meaningful reports, and organisations that use both Excel and Access (or another DBMS) almost interchangeably can feed data between the two applications.

Follow the steps on pages 50–53 for the full click-by-click walkthrough (older Excel — Data tab > Get External Data > From Access, locating a database such as the book's example BakeryDatabase, then using the Select Table dialog to choose one or more tables such as tblCustomers before Import; newer Office 365 — Data tab > Get & Transform Data > Get Data > From Database > From Microsoft Access Database, using the Navigator dialog which for the worked example database shows nine available tables). As with the previous import types, the imported table data can be refreshed later via right-click > Refresh if the connection was kept live.

## LESSON 4 – SHAPING DATA SETS (book pages 55–69)

In this section you will learn how to: remove duplicate data; validate that given values belong to a reference data set using the VLOOKUP function; validate that given values belong to a specific range using one or more IF functions; extract values from a string using text functions.

### 4.1 Shaping Data Sets Introduction (page 56)
To get meaningful information from data analysis, you need to work with a good-quality data set. Data imported from external sources such as text files, spreadsheets, web pages, or databases may contain errors or inconsistencies, or may not be structured the way you need for analysis. You will often need to shape and prepare a data set before you analyse it — this happens in the data preparation phase of the data analysis process (see Lesson 1.3), and before shaping/preparing the original data, it's good practice to save a backup copy so you can recover the unmodified data if a cleaning step goes wrong.

Common cleaning tasks (the specific tasks needed depend on the type and quality of the analysis) include: fixing spelling mistakes; identifying and removing duplicate data; finding and replacing text; fixing incorrect formatting; changing the case of text; removing extra spaces from text; fixing number or date/time formats; merging and splitting columns; transforming and rearranging columns and rows; and reconciling data by comparing or matching values. Spreadsheet applications contain built-in features for some of these cleaning tasks, so it's good practice to use them first — for example, the Spell Checker tool for fixing spelling mistakes, the Find and Replace tool to fix incorrect formatting or replace text with new values, and the Remove Duplicates tool to remove duplicate rows within the data set. Other tasks, like extracting data, may require you to manipulate imported values using formulas, which is where VLOOKUP, IF, and text functions come in.

### 4.2 Removing Duplicate Data (pages 56–58)
Data duplication can occur in data sets when records are entered more than once, or consolidated from multiple sources. Duplicates may occur for some fields or for entire records, and if you want to limit the records to unique data per some fields or unique records overall, you can use Excel's built-in Remove Duplicates tool, which finds and removes exact duplicates.

Follow the steps on pages 56–58 for the full click-by-click walkthrough: it's good practice to first copy the range you want to de-duplicate to another worksheet or workbook before removing duplicates, since the tool permanently deletes duplicate rows; then select any cell within the data set and, on the Data tab, in the Data Tools group, click Remove Duplicates; check or uncheck the specific columns you want to compare for duplication in the Remove Duplicates dialog box (My data has headers should be checked appropriately); click OK, and a confirmation message reports how many duplicate values were found and removed and how many unique values remain. In the worked example (salesperson records with Salesperson ID, name, office location, and sales amount), Excel reported "2 duplicate values found and removed; 6 unique values remain" for a 8-row data set. Note: when duplicates are removed, only the first occurrence of the value in the data set is kept — other identical values are deleted.

### 4.3 Validating Data Using VLOOKUP (pages 58–61)
In Excel you can use the VLOOKUP function to validate data by cross-checking whether a given value exists in a separate reference data set — this can save a huge amount of time when reconciling data. For example, you can use VLOOKUP to verify a value such as a country code by matching it against a list of valid, approved country codes stored in a reference range elsewhere in the workbook; if a match is found, VLOOKUP returns the corresponding value from the reference table (verifying the value is valid), and if no match is found, it returns the #N/A error (flagging the value as invalid or not found). You can also use VLOOKUP to perform calculations or display results using values returned — for example, looking up a commission rate in a table based on a salesperson's ID, then calculating the commission amount to be paid by multiplying that rate by the salesperson's sales value.

The syntax for the VLOOKUP function is: VLOOKUP(lookup_value, table_array, col_index_num, [range_lookup]). lookup_value is the value you want to look for in a specified range of cells. table_array is the range of cells containing the value you want to return. col_index_num is the column number in the specified table_array you want to return a value from (counting from 1 for the leftmost column of the table_array). range_lookup specifies whether you want an exact match (FALSE or omitted... note: the book specifies FALSE for exact match and TRUE for approximate match) or an approximate match (TRUE) — exact match mode is useful when you have a unique key like an ID; approximate match mode is useful when looking up the best/nearest match for a value, for example finding the right commission rate tier for a given sales figure. The VLOOKUP function searches vertically down the first column of the specified table array and then looks across that row to find the specified column and return the value from the cell where that row and column meet.

Follow the steps on pages 60–61 for the full click-by-click walkthrough: with an imported regional sales data set in columns A5:C19 (Country, Country Code, Sales £) that contains some inconsistent country codes, and a reference table listing valid country codes and country names in a separate range (H6:I12), you select an empty cell (cell F6 in the worked example) next to the value you want to check, and type a formula such as =VLOOKUP(B6,$H$6:$I$12,1,FALSE) and press Enter to look up the value in cell B6 within the reference table's first column ($H$6:$I$12 — note the dollar signs make the cell references absolute so they won't change when the formula is copied), returning the code if found or #N/A if not. You then copy the formula down the full range (e.g. F7:F19) to check every row; any country code that doesn't exist in the reference data set will show #N/A, indicating that value is not found/invalid and needs correcting before further analysis.

### 4.4 Validating Data Using IF Functions (pages 62–65)
In Excel you can use the IF function to validate a data set — for example checking whether values fall within a particular expected range. The IF function is one of the most popular and useful functions in Excel for evaluating a condition and returning one value if the condition is met, and another value if it is not.

The syntax for the IF function is: IF(logical_test, [value_if_true], [value_if_false]). logical_test is a value or logical expression that evaluates to TRUE or FALSE. value_if_true is the value to return when logical_test evaluates as TRUE (this argument is optional). value_if_false is the value to return when logical_test evaluates as FALSE (this argument is also optional).

When analysing data, you often need to evaluate several conditions at the same time, which means you need to create more complex logical tests. You can do this by combining an IF function with an AND or OR function: if the logical test contains the AND function, TRUE is returned only if all of the conditions are met (otherwise FALSE); if the logical test contains the OR function, TRUE is returned if any of the conditions are met (otherwise FALSE). You can also use multiple IF functions together to create complex logical tests — multiple IF functions used together in one formula are known as nested IF functions, useful for returning 3 or more different results, and additional IF statements can be included in the value_if_true and value_if_false arguments of the outer IF function.

Follow the steps on pages 63–65 for the full click-by-click walkthrough. A single-condition worked example checks whether sales figures in a data set exceed a maximum value specified in a cell (F6, set to 800,000): selecting cell D6 and typing =IF(B6>$F$6,"Error",B6) returns "Error" if the sales amount exceeds the maximum, otherwise it returns the sales amount itself unchanged; copying the formula down flagged one location (Germany, at 820,000) as exceeding the 800,000 maximum in the worked data set. A two-condition (range-validation) worked example checks values against both a minimum and maximum specified in separate cells (F16 and G16): typing =IF(AND(B16>=$F$16,B16<=$G$16),B16,"Error") into cell D16 and copying down returns "Error" for any value that falls outside the specified minimum–maximum range, otherwise it returns the value unchanged — flagging Germany's value as an error again in that example, since it fell outside the 250,000–800,000 range. A note on page 65 also shows two equivalent nested-IF alternative formulas that achieve the same minimum/maximum range check without using AND, of the general pattern =IF(B16>$G$16,"Error",IF(B16<$F$16,"Error",B16)).

### 4.5 Extracting Values Using Text Functions (pages 66–69)
One of the main tasks in cleaning data is to extract parts of data and parse it into separate columns — for example splitting text in a single cell into multiple columns, such as dividing a full name into separate first- and last-name columns, or splitting an address contained in one cell into several columns.

The common Excel functions used for extracting text are: LEFT(text,[num_chars]) — extracts a specified number of characters from the left side of a string, e.g. =LEFT("IrelandIE",3) returns "Ire"; RIGHT(text,[num_chars]) — extracts a specified number of characters from the right side of a string, e.g. =RIGHT("IrelandIE",3) returns "and" (as per the pattern shown, extracting from the right side); MID(text,start_num,num_chars) — extracts a specified number of characters starting from a specified position in a string, e.g. =MID("Ireland",2,3) returns "rel"; LEN(text) — returns the length of a string as a number of characters, e.g. =LEN("Ireland") returns 7; FIND(find_text, within_text,[start_num]) — returns the position, as a number, of one text string inside another string, e.g. =FIND("land","Ireland",1) returns 4 (case-sensitive; if the text isn't found, FIND returns a #VALUE! error).

These functions are frequently combined (nested) to perform more complex text extraction. Two combinations highlighted in the book: LEFT and LEN together — LEFT(text,LEN(text)-n) — removes the last n characters from a string by first calculating the total string length and then taking everything except the last n characters; this is useful when there are entries of variable length and you want to remove a specified number of characters from the right. LEFT and FIND together — LEFT(text,FIND(character,text)-1) — extracts the characters to the left of a specified character up until (but not including) that character; for example =LEFT("Ireland_IE",FIND("_","Ireland_IE")-1) returns "Ireland".

Follow the steps on pages 66–68 for the full click-by-click walkthrough: with a column of combined "Country" + "Country Code" text values needing separation (e.g. "IrelandIE", "United KingdomGB", "FranceFR"), the country name is extracted using a formula pattern like =LEFT(A4,LEN(A4)-2) (since every country code suffix is exactly 2 characters), copied down the column; the 2-letter country code itself is extracted using =RIGHT(A4,2), copied down; and both extracted columns are then copied and pasted as values (not formulas) into a final "cleansed" output range so the results are static.

### 5.1 Formatting Data Sets as Tables (pages 72–73)
Formatting a data set as a table in Excel helps add functionality to a worksheet and makes managing and analysing data easier. A table is also an excellent source for a pivot table later on. The visual design and layout of a table facilitates the summary and presentation of key numerical data, and Excel provides many predefined (and some custom) table styles to quickly format a table's display.

Before formatting a data set as a table you need to organise the data set following some guidelines: organise the data in rows and columns, with each row containing information about one record, such as a sales entry or inventory transaction; ensure each column contains a descriptive heading in the first row of the data set; ensure each column contains one consistent type of data, such as dates, currency, or text; ensure there are no blank rows and no completely blank columns.

## LESSON 6 – PIVOT TABLE DATA AGGREGATION (book pages 79–96)

In this section you will learn how to: change the method of aggregation for a value; display multiple aggregation values; use built-in calculations.

### 6.1 Summarising Data Introduction (page 80)
Once you have prepared your data, you can start analysing it. In Excel, you can perform basic analysis using statistical methods like sum, count, average, maximum, and minimum over a selected range, and various sorting/filtering/table tricks (see Lesson 5) let you ask simple questions of the data. But sometimes you need more powerful analysis to answer broader questions and spot comparisons, patterns, and trends — questions such as "which store sold the most units this month?", "what percentage of the year's sales took place in the first quarter?", or "how did this year's sales compare with last year's?" A pivot table is a powerful tool for summarising and analysing large data sets with thousands of rows quickly and easily, letting you find the number of units sold both by store and by month at a glance, and there are many ways you can work with pivot tables to analyse large data sets for interesting patterns.

### 6.2 Changing Aggregation Methods (pages 80–83)
A pivot table presents a summary of the underlying source data by applying summary functions to the data. By default, the data in a pivot table is summed, but you can change the way the data is summarised to suit the purpose of your analysis — you do this by changing the summary function, also known as the aggregation method. Excel's built-in aggregate/summary functions available in a pivot table are: Sum (adds up the values — the default aggregation method for numeric fields); Count (the number of values — the default for text/non-numeric fields); Average (the mean/average value); Max (the largest value); Min (the lowest/smallest value).

Follow the steps on pages 80–83 for the full click-by-click walkthrough: with a pivot table already summarising total credit-card transaction amount by department, click any value in the "Sum of Amount" field of the PivotTable, then on the Analyze tab, in the Active Field group, click Field Settings; in the Value Field Settings dialog, select the function Average in the "Summarize value field by" list box; type a new name such as "Average Amount" into the Custom Name text box (a note explains every field in a pivot table has a name by default inherited from the source data heading, and if you don't rename the field manually after changing its function, Excel will automatically append the function name, e.g. changing to Average would default the name to "Average of Amount"). You can also click Number Format within the same dialog to format the resulting values, e.g. setting the category to Currency with 0 decimal places, before clicking OK twice to close both dialogs. A quicker alternative method shown alongside is to right-click directly on a value in the pivot table, choose Summarize Values By, and pick a function from the flyout submenu (Sum, Count, Average, Max, Min, Product, or More Options) to switch the aggregation method instantly. In the worked example this changed a table of Sum of Amount by department (Appliances $2,381; Food $2,042; Furniture $2,325; Hardware $1,424; Housewares $2,149; Ladies $3,928; Linens $1,492; Men $1,942; Toys $1,207; Grand Total $18,890) into an Average Amount view (Appliances $104; Food $102; Furniture $111; Hardware $84; Housewares $93; Ladies $91; Linens $75; Men $102; Toys $67; Grand Total $93).

### 6.3 Displaying Multiple Aggregation Values (pages 84–85)
For an overview of different summary values on a data set, you can display multiple summary/aggregation calculations in a pivot table at the same time — for example you may want to analyse the sales performance of multiple stores and compare several aspects at once, such as the total amount of sales by store, the total number of sales by store, the average sale amount by store, the highest sale amount by store, and the lowest sale amount by store — you can display all these aggregation values using Sum, Count, Average, Max, and Min calculations at the same time in a pivot table.

Follow the steps on pages 84–85 for the full click-by-click walkthrough: to display multiple aggregation values, drag and drop the same field (e.g. "Amount") repeatedly from the PivotTable Fields panel into the Σ Values area multiple times — each instance you drop automatically displays as Sum aggregate by default; you then right-click each instance's field individually and choose Summarize Values By to change its function — for example changing one copy to Max and another to Min. After changing functions, right-click each of the four value fields and select Value Field Settings to rename each with a clear custom name, e.g. renaming "Sum of Amount" to "Total," "Count of Trans #" to "Transactions," "Max of Amount2" to "Highest," and "Min of Amount3" to "Lowest" — giving a single labelled pivot table with four side-by-side aggregation columns per department (in the worked example: Appliances — Total $2,381, Transactions 23, Highest $189, Lowest 2; Food — Total $2,042, Transactions 20, Highest $200, Lowest 20; and so on down to a Grand Total row).

### 6.4 Using Built-In Calculations (pages 86–93)
The PivotTable command contains built-in calculations, also known as custom calculations, that you can use to quickly add a pivot table view of values presented in different ways, such as percentage calculations, difference calculations, running values, and ranked values. For example, when analysing the sales performance of multiple stores, you might want to show the difference between two years' sales totals for each store, to see any trends, or you might want to see how the current running total builds up over time to see if you're on track to hit a target. This functionality is provided in the pivot table's "Show Values As" option.

The common built-in (custom) calculations available under Show Values As are: No Calculation (displays the original value, unmodified); % of Grand Total (displays values as a percentage of the grand total of all values/data points across the whole pivot table); % of Column Total (displays all the values in each column or series as a percentage of the total for that column/series); % of Row Total (displays the value in each row or category as a percentage of the total for that row/category); Difference From (displays values as the difference between two values in a pivot table — for example, the difference between this year's sales value and last year's sales value for the same item, using a designated Base field and Base item to subtract from); % Difference From (displays values as a percentage of the difference between two values, again using a Base field and Base item — for example, the percentage change in sales value between two years, which is a useful figure for examining trend data); Running Total In (displays cumulative or running totals of values over a period of time, such as cumulative sales value over the year-to-date, up to and including each row's date); Rank Smallest to Largest (displays the rank of selected values in a specific field, listing the smallest item in the field as 1, and each larger value with a higher rank number); Rank Largest to Smallest (displays the rank of selected values in a specific field, listing the largest item in the field as 1, and each smaller value with a higher rank number).

A further worked example uses Difference From with the Base field set to Payment Type and the Base item set to Visa, to compare each other card type's total transaction amount against the Visa total (the worked result showed Amex at −$12,601, Debit at −$13,096, and MasterCard at −$11,419 relative to Visa's $14,002 total, out of an $18,890 grand total) — the note explains that the Difference From calculation subtracts one value from another based on the value in the Base field, and shows the result (positive or negative) relative to the Base item, and that switching to % Difference From instead shows this same comparison expressed as a percentage figure, which is useful for trend analysis. Every custom calculation can also be applied more quickly via the same right-click > Show Values As context-menu route described in section 6.2.

### 7.1 Grouping Date, Time, and Numeric Data (pages 98–101)
When analysing large sets of data in pivot tables, you may want to look at subsets of the data to find patterns and trends. You can do this by organizing the data into groups. ## LESSON 8 – FILTERING PIVOT TABLES (book pages 107–122)

In this section you will learn how to: use report filters; use slicers; use timelines.

### 8.1 Using Report Filters (pages 108–111)
When you have summarised data in a pivot table in Excel, you may want to create reports that analyse certain parts of the data. You can create these reports using one or more report filters. A report filter can be used to display selected items of the pivot table and hide others; items selected in the filter are displayed above the pivot table for easy access, and items not selected are hidden. For example, you might want to show the sales amounts for specific years instead of all years, or show the sales amounts for specific regions instead of all regions.

You need to create a pivot table report for each unique combination you want to see — you can do this by creating a report filter, filtering by each region or item you want, and copying the output to a new worksheet for each item; a quicker way to do this is to create a report filter and use the Show Report Filter Pages feature, which automates the process of applying a filter for each item and copying the results into separate new worksheets automatically for each item, saving lots of time, especially if you're creating a large number of reports.

Follow the steps on pages 108–111 for the full click-by-click walkthrough: with a pivot table already showing total sales by salesperson (and year/region across columns), drag the field you want to filter by (Region in the worked example) into the Filters area of the PivotTable Fields panel — the Account Date and Region filters now appear above the pivot table, initially both set to (All); to view a specific combination, select the desired value from each filter's drop-down (e.g. 2017 from the Account Date filter, and United States from the Region filter) and click OK for each — the pivot table now shows just the filtered subset (in the worked example, total sales for United States salespeople in 2017 came to $86,178.20 across five listed salespeople). To clear the filters, select (All) again from each filter's drop-down. To generate a separate report for every item in a filter automatically: on the Analyze tab, in the PivotTable group, click the Options drop-down arrow, and click Show Report Filter Pages; in the dialog, select the field to generate pages for (e.g. Region) and click OK — Excel then creates a new, separately-named worksheet for every unique item in that field, each containing a copy of the pivot table pre-filtered to just that one item. A note explains that if changes are later made to the original pivot table, those changes will not automatically be updated in the separately-generated report-filter-page worksheets.

### 8.2 Using Pivot Table Slicers (pages 112–116)
As well as using report filters, you can use slicers to visually and interactively filter data in a pivot table (recall Lesson 5.2 covered slicers for regular Excel tables — the same slicer concept applies to pivot tables, and provides visual filter buttons that make it easy to see and click which items are included in a filtered view). Slicers also let you customise their appearance — you can change the name of the slicer, the header displayed, the order in which items are displayed, the colour of the slicer, the number of columns of buttons, and the button size.

Follow the steps on pages 112–116 for the full click-by-click walkthrough: open the workbook and go to the relevant worksheet; on the Analyze tab, in the Filter group, click Insert Slicer; in the Insert Slicers dialog box, select the relevant field(s) — the worked example selects Area — and click OK; the slicer appears alongside the pivot table (note: if there are multiple slicers, they display in a layered/stacked arrangement by default), and you can drag it to reposition, and use its sizing handles to resize; click the buttons within the slicer to filter the pivot table by single item, or select contiguous multiple items by holding Shift and clicking the first and last item, or select multiple non-contiguous items by holding Ctrl and clicking each one; click the Clear Filter icon at the top-right of the slicer to clear the filter. To change slicer settings: on the Options tab, in the Slicer group, click Slicer Settings; in the dialog you can change the Name and Caption (the display header text), and set Item Sorting and Filtering options (ascending/descending order, whether to hide items with no data, whether to show items deleted from the data source last, etc.). ### 8.3 Using Timelines (pages 117–121)
To easily display data in a pivot table for different time periods, you can use a Timeline. Similar to slicers, you can customise and format Timelines, and you can use a single Timeline to filter numerous pivot tables at once, as long as the pivot tables share the same underlying data source. Follow the steps on pages 117–120 for the full click-by-click walkthrough: select any value in the pivot table, then on the Analyze tab, in the Filter group, click Insert Timeline; in the Insert Timelines dialog box, check the date-formatted field you want to base the timeline on (e.g. Date) and click OK (note: you can add multiple timelines at once by selecting multiple date-formatted fields); drag the timeline to position it above the pivot table. To select the specific time period you want to display, click and drag either end of the highlighted tile-selection bar within the timeline — the worked example narrows a full year down to a specific mid-March date range. On the Options tab, in the Show group, you can toggle on/off the Header, Scrollbar, Selection Label, and Time Level elements displayed on the timeline control. To clear the applied time filter, click the small Clear Filter icon at the top-right of the timeline. To delete a Timeline, select it, press Delete, or right-click and choose Remove Timeline. To connect a single Timeline across multiple pivot tables that share the same data source, use the same Report Connections dialog (Options tab > Report Connections) described for slicers in 8.2.

## LESSON 10 – DATA VISUALIZATION TOOLS (book pages 139–150)

In this section you will learn how to: understand the concept of data visualization using reports and dashboards; outline common visualizations; recognise common data visualization tools and their functions; setup tools for data visualizations; import data from a spreadsheet into a data visualization tool.

### 10.1 Key Features of Data Visualization Tools (pages 140–141)
Data visualization provides a visual way to access data by displaying trends and patterns within data in a visual form. Data visualization makes it simpler and more efficient to analyse and understand textual and numerical data; it saves time; it provides insights that contribute to business intelligence; and it helps with decision making. It can be used in the earlier stages of data analytics to explore and make sense of the data, as well as for reporting purposes.

There are lots of specialized data visualization tools designed to help businesses easily perform sophisticated data analytics. Key features of specialized data visualization tools include connecting to a wide range of data sources including spreadsheets and databases, as well as non-traditional sources such as web pages and images; the ability to create data sets (collections of data) based on one source or based on data from multiple sources combined and filtered; the ability to create a simple data set based on a single table from a spreadsheet, or you can create a data set which is a combination of data from a website table and a spreadsheet table, for example, blending data showing results of a social media marketing campaign.

These powerful tools allow you to explore data sets and find and present insights into your business needs through the creation of data visualizations. There are a wide range of data visualizations provided that can go beyond traditional forms; part of your data analysis will include determining which types of visualizations provide the best insights and business intelligence for your specific needs. Common examples of data visualizations include: Charts (column, bar, line, pie) — useful for displaying relationships among numeric data by category or over time periods, for example a line chart tracking quantity by month and product category, or a donut chart showing web-browser market share; Tables — useful for presenting and comparing detailed data and exact values, for example a full product-sales table listing detailed order counts and revenue by product; Key Performance Indicators (KPIs, and gauge charts) — useful for providing key indicators for highlighting critical data and measuring performance using predefined conditions, for example a "Sales Target by Year" KPI card showing 1.98M achieved against a goal, or an equivalent gauge chart showing the same target visually as a dial reading; Maps — useful for presenting the distribution of data across geographic locations, for example a sales-volume map with proportionally-sized bubbles located across different US states.

Data visualization tools go beyond just presenting information in visual forms and also provide interactive capabilities that allow you to filter and drill into the data in visualizations for querying and analysis. Data visualization tools also enable production of insightful visual reports from the data analysis process. A basic report includes visualizations and results from the data analysis process; a report can consist of a single page with one or more visualizations, or it can consist of multiple pages of visualizations — the aim of reports is to provide key insights into your data that can be used to achieve business goals, such as summary information, trends, and patterns in a visual way. As well as reports, data visualization tools also let you combine multiple visualizations to display key information and metrics via dashboards — dashboards provide consolidated visual displays allowing information to be monitored at a glance on a single screen, and this information is updated in real-time as the underlying data changes; the aim of dashboards is to provide insights into your business goals, and they tend to be built to visualise and organise data for the purpose of monitoring changes in real time. Data visualization tools also provide the ability to easily share the business intelligence information produced in dashboards and reports, and they have the ability to support collaboration.

With a large collection of data connectors and visualizations with intuitive design, it is one of the leading tools in data visualization — each visual comes with shareable code that allows you to tweet it, share it on Facebook, or embed it in your organisation's website. ### 11.2 Creating Chart Visualizations (pages 154–159)
Charts are one of the most commonly used visualizations in data analytics. They reveal relationships such as similarities, differences, and trends among multiple values. Microsoft Power BI has a range of visualization options for charts to more advanced visuals. Some popular types of charts include: Line chart — this is the most popular chart type and is used to present a continuous data set, and is best suited for trend-based visualizations of data over time, with an emphasis on the continuation or flow of the values (a trend); Pie/donut chart — a pie chart typically represents numbers in percentages, and is used to visualize a part-to-whole relationship or a composition; similarly, a donut chart shows the relationship of parts to a whole, allowing for space in the centre for a label or icon; Bar/column chart — a bar chart is a horizontal column chart, best suited for multiple categories, especially those with lengthy labels or for displaying negative numbers, whereas a column chart, on the other hand, displays data in vertical columns. To facilitate comparison of multiple variables across categories of data, Power BI includes Stacked bar and Stacked column charts (which can be used to create simple bar/column charts too), as well as Clustered bar and Clustered column charts.

When deciding which chart type to use, consider: the purpose of the chart (is it for comparison, composition, distribution, or relationship?); the categories of data to be presented (are there single or multiple categories? are they over a time period, or among groups?); the data points to be presented (how many are there for each category?). When you create your chart in Power BI, you can then modify how it looks using the formatting options in Power BI.

### 11.6 Creating Visualizations Using Maps (pages 177–182)

### 12.1 Creating Visualizations to Measure Progress (pages 184–192)
In Microsoft Power BI you can create visualizations to measure and visually communicate progress towards a set goal or target. The two main types of visualizations for measuring progress are the KPI (key performance indicator) visualization and the Gauge visualization.

The KPI visualization displays the Base measure (current status), the Goal to be achieved, and the distance from the goal as a percentage. For example, the Base measure may be the current number of customers, and the Goal may be the number of customers you'd like to achieve this year, evaluated against the Goal. If the Base measure exceeds the Goal, this is a positive KPI, shown in green font with a tick. If the Base measure isn't meeting the Goal, this is a negative KPI, shown in red font with an exclamation mark. If the Base measure matches the goal exactly, the font is shown in yellow.

The Gauge visualization is also known as a gauge chart. This has a circular arc measuring progress toward a goal/target. All possible values are spread evenly along the arc, from the minimum (left-most value) to the maximum (right-most value). The goal/target is represented by a marked line on the gauge. Progress (current status) toward the goal/target is represented by the shading. A single value shown in bold inside the arc represents the progress.

Follow the steps on pages 186–190 for the full click-by-click walkthrough of creating a KPI visualization measuring a Customer's KPI (showing the number of existing customers against a targeted number of customers for the year) and an Orders KPI (showing the number of orders against a targeted number of orders for the year): open the relevant Power BI file; to set the target for goal achievement, click New Measure on the Home tab, in the Calculations group; in the Formula bar that appears at the top of the canvas, type a DAX-style measure such as "Customers Target = 1800" and press Enter to add the measure to the Fields list; in Report view, click on a blank area of the Report Canvas and select the KPI icon in the Visualizations pane; drag and drop the field Customer ID from the Fields pane into the Value (base) area of the Visualizations pane (the value controls the base value's display units and decimal places); drag and drop the field Order Date from the Fields pane into the Trend axis area (the trend axis is displayed as the background of the KPI visualization) — clicking the Order Date drop-down list in the Trend axis area and selecting Date Hierarchy; drag and drop the measure Customers Target from the Fields pane into the Target (goal) area — the visualization now displays the goal and the distance from the goal as a percentage; click the Count of Customer ID field in the Value area of the Visualizations pane and change the aggregation to Count (Distinct) — the indicator will display in green with a check icon if the target exceeds the goal, and in red with an exclamation icon if the target is not yet met. ### 13.5 Good Design Practice (pages 224–225)
When you are creating reports and dashboards, your aim is to present key insights and actionable information that can be understood and used effectively. To do this, you need to determine the data that you need to gather and what type of visualizations to use. You need to consider the best way to present the data to your audience, and you will need to think carefully about the design and layout of your reports and dashboards. Incorporating good design practices into your reports and dashboards will make them easier to understand and therefore more effective. Good design practices simplify the visual representation of complex data, helping others understand the key insights and information more easily. Some tips for good design include:

Use a clean and uncluttered layout — a clean and uncluttered layout on reports and dashboards means that the reader can see the key insights easily; presenting only the most relevant data as a cluttered layout can confuse and distract the user from key insights (the book contrasts a cluttered "Corporate Summary Report" layout against a clean, clutter-free dashboard example to illustrate this point).

Use descriptive titles and labels — use descriptive titles to help readers understand what your data visualizations show; also consider adding descriptive text labels using text boxes to describe the page, grouping of visuals, or to describe an individual visual.

Use consistent fonts and color — use consistent fonts and colors to help the reader understand the information being presented. In general use one or two font types and up to three font sizes to create a visual hierarchy for titles, labels, and data; using different font sizes helps differentiate headings from actual data.

Use color for emphasis and understanding — use color appropriately to help emphasise critical data and improve the reader's understanding of the visuals. The appropriate use of color can help make connections between visuals and make data processing easier. When applying colors to reports and dashboards consider the following: displaying most of the data using natural colors; using bright or dark colors to highlight outliers or critical data; using color shades or gradients to show the spread of values across a range; using color to highlight variance around a central value — for example, using green to show positive values and red to show negative values.

Avoid multiple pages — where possible, aim to divide the visualizations over fewer pages, rather than spreading them across many pages, since a reader who has to click through multiple pages to compare data may lose their sense of context, and this could add unnecessary complexity and hinder the reader from getting the key insights and information more efficiently.

### 13.6 Evaluating Results (page 226)
With the results of the data analytical processes easily accessible through reports and dashboards, you and your team can collaborate on the evaluation of the results in the evaluation phase of the data analysis process (recall Lesson 1.3's six-phase model — evaluation is phase 5, sitting between Modelling and Deployment). Some questions you might want to raise in the evaluation process include: what insights have you gained from the process? Do the insights address the problem you set out to solve or the goals you set out to achieve? What are the practical implementation steps that can be taken to address the problem or achieve the goals? What might be the next data analytical phase? If you find that you haven't solved the original problem or met the original objectives, you can revisit and review the business understanding and data understanding phases to make the most of the on-going data analysis. The key to gaining more insight is not always getting more data, but gathering the right data. And the dynamic nature of business and its continuous development means that the process of data analysis is always ongoing — data analytics is best thought of as a continuous, cyclical process rather than a one-off project with a fixed endpoint.

### 13.7 Review Exercise (page 227)
This knowledge and practical review covers: which method is used for sharing analytical reports (Power BI service); publishing a file to My Workspace (with a note reminding you that you can only share the file with another user if you both have Power BI Pro licenses); signing out from the Power BI service; identifying which design practice should be avoided to achieve a clean, uncluttered layout in your reports and dashboards (avoid dividing visualizations over multiple pages, avoid using white space to section headers and text, avoid decorative elements such as background images, or align and distribute the visualizations); and why you should use consistent fonts and colors when designing reports and dashboards (to differentiate between the headings and actual data, to accentuate a hierarchical structure, to emphasize critical data, or to show the availability of various options). It also includes a further hands-on exercise using a PivotReport Power BI file, applying Background color conditional formatting to an Amount column with three colour bands, and Data bars conditional formatting to a Transactions column with a purple bar colour and right-to-left bar direction — mirroring the practice from the Lesson 11 review exercise, reinforcing that these formatting skills recur throughout the second half of the course.
